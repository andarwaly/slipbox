# Sun Gating and Morning Light

You have probably heard someone recommend getting outside first thing in the morning
"to fix your sleep." At Banner Health, patients ask our providers about this often
enough that we wanted to explain what is actually happening in the body, and how to do
it safely — because the practice people are describing, sometimes called "sun gating,"
is easy to confuse with something else entirely: staring directly at the sun, or "sun
gazing," which is not something we recommend and can damage your eyes. Sun gating does
not carry that risk. The two practices are often mentioned in the same breath online,
so we want to be clear about the difference before going any further.

## What Sun Gating Actually Is

Sun gating is simply spending time outdoors in natural light shortly after waking,
without staring at the sun itself. The point is ordinary outdoor brightness reaching
your eyes at an angle, ambient light while you walk, stretch, or drink your coffee on
the porch. Sun gazing, by contrast, means looking directly at the sun's disk, which can
cause lasting retinal damage. Sun gating carries none of that risk, because the practice
never asks you to look at the sun at all.

## Why the Morning Window Matters

Your body's circadian clock is set largely by light signals reaching the eyes, and the
research on this points to the first 30 to 60 minutes after waking as the window in
which the body is most sensitive to light. A light signal arriving in that window has
an outsized influence on when your internal clock thinks "day" has started, which
cascades into when it later expects "night" to begin.

## Indoors Doesn't Really Substitute

A common question we get is whether standing near a bright window, or turning on a lot
of indoor lights, accomplishes the same thing. It does not, at least not as well.
Outdoor light is still brighter than indoor lighting and much more effective at
adjusting your body clock. Even an overcast morning sky is far more intense than a
well-lit living room. If getting outdoors genuinely isn't possible, indoor light is
better than no light at all, but it is a fallback, not an equivalent.

## What About Glasses and Contacts?

People often ask whether they need to remove their glasses or contacts to get the
benefit. You don't need to. For most people, clear contact lenses and regular glasses
are usually fine when getting morning light — they don't block enough light to meaningfully
change the effect. The exception is anything that blocks light on purpose: sunglasses,
photochromic lenses that have darkened, or anything else designed to cut down brightness.

## Beyond Everyday Habit: Clinical Use

Morning light exposure isn't only a wellness habit — it has an established clinical
role. Light therapy, often using a timed morning light box, is used by sleep
specialists to treat circadian rhythm disorders, jet lag, and delayed sleep phase
syndrome, where a patient's internal clock runs persistently later than a typical
schedule requires. In these clinical settings, timing and intensity are prescribed more
precisely than the casual "step outside" advice above, but the underlying mechanism is
the same one this article describes.

## Bottom Line

Getting outside soon after waking — sun gating, not sun gazing — gives your circadian
clock a strong, safe morning signal. Ordinary glasses or clear contacts won't get in the
way. Indoor light is a distant second choice. And for people with a diagnosed rhythm
disorder, this same mechanism is already the basis of a real clinical treatment, not
just a lifestyle tip.
