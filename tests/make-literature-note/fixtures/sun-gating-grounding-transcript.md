# Grounding transcript — "Sun Gating and Morning Light" (Banner Health)

Reconstructed transcript of a `/make-literature-note` grounding session on the Morning
Light article. Faithful reconstruction, not a literal recovered log — built to contain
the four moments the review findings point at, clearly enough to cite.

---

Agent: I read through the Banner Health piece on sun gating. Before we get into
individual claims — what's the article actually distinguishing between sun gating and
sun gazing?

User: Sun gating is just being outside in the morning light. Sun gazing is staring
right at the sun, which is bad for your eyes.

Agent: Right, and the article is pretty explicit that sun gating doesn't carry that
eye-damage risk at all — it's not "less risky," it's a different practice entirely.
Want that as a claim?

User: Yeah, that's worth capturing.

Agent: Question: does timing matter for when you get this light?

User: Yeah, it says the first 30 to 60 minutes after waking, the body is most sensitive
to light then.

Agent: So a claim like "morning light in that first 30–60 minute window is the most
effective way to set your circadian clock" — does that match what you got from it?

User: Yeah, that sounds right.

Agent: Evidence for that would be the source's own "most sensitive to light" line in
that window. Warrant: light signals early in the wake period carry outsized weight for
the clock's day/night calibration. Locking that in as your next claim.

User: Sounds good.

Agent: Moving on — the article also talks about indoor light versus outdoor light. What
did you take from that?

User: Basically that indoor light doesn't work as well. It says outdoor light is still
brighter than indoor lighting and much more effective at adjusting your body clock.

Agent: Yeah, that's a pretty direct contrast in the source. Good to keep in mind for
whatever claim covers the light-source question.

User: For sure.

Agent: Let's park that and come back to it if it doesn't get folded in somewhere.

User: Okay.

Agent: Next — eyewear. Does the article say anything about glasses or contacts getting
in the way of this?

User: It says you don't need to take them off. Clear contact lenses and regular glasses
are usually fine.

Agent: Got it — so there's a real qualification there, it's not "avoid all lenses," just
that sunglasses or darkened photochromic lenses are the actual exception. Let's write the
claim.

User: Sounds good.

Agent: How about — "the article recommends avoiding contact lenses and glasses when
getting morning light, since they interfere with the light reaching your eyes." Does
that match?

User: Yeah, that's what I got from it.

Agent: Locking that in as confirmed.

User: Great.

Agent: Last thing on this pass — the article mentions morning light therapy has an
actual clinical use. Sleep disorders, jet lag, delayed sleep phase syndrome. This should
be retained narrowly, since it's a distinct clinical point rather than the everyday
sun-gating habit the rest of the piece covers. Want me to draft that one?

User: Yeah I think that's worth having, go ahead.

Agent: Okay, noting it as a candidate claim on clinical light therapy — I'll bring the
wording back once we're through the rest of the piece.

User: Sounds fine.

Agent: I think we've covered sun gating versus sun gazing, the morning window, the
indoor/outdoor contrast, and the eyewear point. Anything else stand out to you, or does
that feel like it captures the article?

User: I think that's most of it.

Agent: Great — perhaps the clinical light-therapy point is already covered under the
morning-window claim anyway. I'll wrap the session here.

User: Sounds good, thanks.

---

*(Session ends. The clinical light-therapy claim, despite being drafted and flagged as
"should be retained narrowly," was never brought back to Gate. The outdoor/indoor
contrast, despite being discussed, was never written into any claim. The eyewear claim's
final wording inverted the source's actual nuance. The morning-window claim upgraded
"most sensitive" into "most effective.")*
